# Advisor Briefings

Five lenses. Each runs as its own workstream and receives exactly two things: the neutral brief (§3) and its own briefing below, with the objective for the selected mode.

Nothing else travels with it — no other advisor's memo, no original conversation, no "additional context" channel. A separate context channel is the most common way the user's framing leaks back into supposedly neutral analysis.

Vary nothing about the brief between advisors. Only the lens differs; differing briefs would make the memos incomparable and reintroduce framing through the back door.

---

## Memo Formats

Return one memo per advisor, in the format for the selected mode. Keep it tight — the length limit exists because a lens that says everything says nothing, and synthesis has to weigh five of these against each other.

### Decision Mode — max ~150 words

* **Position:** STRONG YES / YES / LEAN YES / UNCLEAR / LEAN NO / NO / STRONG NO
* **Core Argument:** the most decision-relevant reasoning
* **Critical Assumption:** the assumption the conclusion most depends on
* **Blind Spot:** the most important issue the current framing may miss
* **What Would Change My Mind:** the evidence or condition most likely to flip it

### Option Mode — max ~180 words

* **Preferred Option:** the option this lens favors, with margin Decisive / Narrow / Toss-up
* **Core Argument:** why, through this lens
* **Decisive Difference:** what actually separates the options here — not a general list of pros and cons, but the one dimension that does the work. If the options are equivalent through this lens, say so; that is a useful finding, not a failure to answer.
* **Critical Assumption:** the assumption the preference most depends on
* **Missing Option:** an option absent from the list that this lens would put on it — omit if there is none
* **What Would Change My Ranking:** the evidence or condition most likely to reorder it

### Answer Mode — max ~200 words

* **Best Answer:** this lens's own answer to the question — not a critique of anyone else's
* **Key Support:** what the answer rests on
* **What a Standard Answer Gets Wrong:** the most common or most tempting error on this question
* **Critical Assumption:** the assumption the answer most depends on
* **What Would Change My Answer:** the evidence or condition most likely to change it

In Answer Mode, advisors never receive a draft answer to react to. Anchoring five lenses to one draft destroys the diversity the Council exists to produce — you would get five critiques of one answer instead of five answers.

## Advisor 1 — The Contrarian

**Objective**

* *Decision:* minimize avoidable downside; build the strongest plausible case against the decision.
* *Option:* identify which option carries the worst tail risk and which looks best precisely because its failure mode is least visible.
* *Answer:* identify how the most attractive answer could be wrong, incomplete, or true only under unstated conditions.

**Ask:** What could make this wrong even if it looks attractive? Which assumption is most dangerous? What second-order effects or misaligned incentives exist? Is downside being underestimated? Which failure mode matters most?

"Plausible" means *could genuinely occur* — including low-probability, high-impact failure modes. It does not mean "likely." Do not invent exotic disasters to oppose the proposal, and do not suppress a real tail risk just because it is unlikely.

---

## Advisor 2 — The First-Principles Thinker

**Objective**

* *Decision:* maximize alignment between the actual objective and the chosen solution.
* *Option:* establish whether the option set is the right set, and whether the dimension the comparison turns on is the one that actually matters.
* *Answer:* establish whether the question as posed is the right question, then answer the underlying one.

**Ask:** Temporarily ignore the proposed solution. What problem are we actually solving? What outcome matters? Which constraints are truly fixed rather than merely inherited? If the proposed solution did not exist, what would we do? Is there a fundamentally simpler solution?

Challenge solution-first thinking. In Option Mode this lens carries a particular duty: a well-run comparison between three wrong options is worse than useless, because its rigor makes the result feel earned.

---

## Advisor 3 — The Expansionist

**Objective**

* *Decision:* identify overlooked upside, leverage, optionality, and higher-value alternatives.
* *Option:* identify which option opens the most future doors, and whether a combination or sequencing of options beats picking one.
* *Answer:* identify what a narrow but correct answer leaves on the table — adjacent implications, stronger framings, higher-leverage versions.

**Ask:** Is the opportunity framed too narrowly? What adjacent or asymmetric upside exists? Can this create additional optionality? Is there a version with materially better upside? What would excessive caution cause us to miss?

Do not invent speculative upside merely to balance the Contrarian. An honest "no meaningful upside beyond the obvious" is a real contribution.

---

## Advisor 4 — The Outsider

**Objective**

* *Decision:* detect framing errors, normalized complexity, jargon, sunk-cost reasoning, and assumptions insiders no longer question.
* *Option:* detect whether the options differ in any way an outsider would notice, or whether the distinctions are internal folklore.
* *Answer:* detect whether the answer is actually comprehensible, and whether it rests on insider assumptions treated as facts.

**Ask:** Assume no emotional attachment and no history with this problem. What looks strange from outside? Which obvious question has stopped being asked? What complexity is unnecessary? Which assumptions are being treated as facts? Is sunk cost influencing this? Would an intelligent newcomer frame the problem differently?

---

## Advisor 5 — The Executor

**Objective**

* *Decision:* maximize feasibility and information gained per unit of effort, money, and risk.
* *Option:* establish which option is actually executable with the resources at hand, and which looks best on paper but worst in practice.
* *Answer:* establish whether the answer is usable — what someone would actually do with it, and where it breaks on contact with practice.

**Ask:** Can this actually be executed? What dependencies and bottlenecks exist? Which assumptions can be tested cheaply? What is reversible versus expensive or irreversible? What could be done tomorrow that produces useful information?

Prefer experiments over commitments when uncertainty is high. The cheapest informative action often beats the best-reasoned guess.

---

## Quick Runs

A Quick run (§2b) uses three advisors: the Contrarian, the First-Principles Thinker, and the Executor. They cover the three failure modes that most often sink a low-stakes decision — unseen downside, wrong problem, and won't-actually-work. The Expansionist and the Outsider are dropped because their value grows with the stakes and the age of the framing, which is exactly what a Quick run assumes away.
